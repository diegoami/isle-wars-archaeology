## Answer

Every source below is `static:decompile` of `Islepro.exe`, sha256 `c4d7a248646454adeb48232e3ac7b3d2c0f08b290051a79d6fe6e47bb76b594e`; scripts are cited as in `questions/pro-static.md` (kind, number, name when it has one, handler). Nothing is quoted: the rules are described in our words (rule 18). "The computer" is the code the computer-turn frame runs for a seat marked computer in the four-seat opponent table. Globals that recur: the seat whose turn it is; the 46-entry ownership table and the 46-entry army table; the four per-seat country lists; the five border tables (each country's neighbours, up to five); the nine island member tables and the per-country island-number table; the 23-country key list the code names VIPC; the list of islands the seat owns completely; the three production-centre countries; the capture flag of the turn; and the pool of armies left to place. A "draw of 1 to n" is how Director's random function is read; the generator itself is Director's runtime (T18) and no distribution is claimed.

**In one paragraph.** The computer's whole turn runs inside one frame script: the shared turn advance and income, its card check and trade-in, its placement, a drawn number of attack rounds, one move, and a card draw when it captured. Placement scores its front-line countries against a ranking of the nine islands by the share of the computer's own countries in each, seeds one army into each threatened key country of every island it owns completely (sometimes also into owned production centres), and divides the rest of the pool by drawn rules among the five best-scored countries — always spending everything, and leaving the pool global itself unzeroed (F013's finding, confirmed). Attacks run in 2 to 5 drawn rounds (more when the computer is large, four more once no human seat is left, fewer when it is small — and a seat below 8 countries has its whole total replaced, so the additions drop with it — and the whole block skipped one turn in 22 while it holds 18 or fewer countries): each round builds the list of acceptable attacks — a front-line country that beats a drawn floor by a margin drawn once per source over a given enemy neighbour — weights same-island pairs double (fourfold on the one island that placement ranked 4), and draws one pair to battle. After the rounds it moves once: its largest safe interior stack walks all but one army along a hard-coded table that maps each of the 23 non-key countries to a neighbouring country, and five one-in-seven corridor fallbacks can instead move from a key country. Its dice, income and card values are the human's; only its choices differ. The difficulty setting (the options screen's level) changes none of this: its only in-game effect is how many countries the human seat is dealt at the start.

### Placement: where the new armies go

#### Q-AI-05 How does the computer choose the countries to place its new armies on, and how does it split the pool?

- **As read (about 85%).** One handler does all of it, in the turn-start frame, after the card check has stored its match. In order:
  - **The head.** If the card check found a set, the pool first receives its effect: the +5, +8 and +15 kinds add that number; the doubling kind doubles the whole pool; the bomb kind adds nothing and runs the bombing branch — nine drawn samples over the enemy countries that border the computer's own, whose comparison and anti-bomb test are F014's reading, confirmed here line by line (the sampled country's army count is used as an index into the army table for the comparison, and the sampled country is the one kept as the victim). F014's "what the nine samples leave at runtime is unestablished" stands.
  - **The candidates.** The shared candidate-list handler builds two lists: the front-line countries (the computer's countries with at least one enemy neighbour) and — by aliasing, not copying — the computer's complete country list. The scoring walks the complete list, not the front-line one: for each of the nine islands it counts how many of the island's countries the computer holds and divides by the island's size (3, 5, 5, 10, 3, 7, 3, 7, 3), giving the computer's share of each island. This is a refinement of F013's wording, which had the priorities reading the list of islands owned; the owned-islands list enters later, in the defence branches.
  - **The island ranking.** Four fixed passes then hand out ranks: the highest share gets 5, the next distinct share 4, the next 3, the next 2; equal shares are ranked together, and a passed island is marked with a negative sentinel so later passes skip it. Because a pass takes the current maximum, later passes revisit an island ranked earlier, and the final ranks depend on how many distinct positive shares there are: with one, that island ends ranked 3 and every untouched island ranked 2; with two, the top island ends ranked 2, the second 4 and the untouched ones 3; with exactly three, the top three get 5, 4 and 3 and the untouched ones 2; with four or more, the top three get 5, 4 and 3, the fourth distinct share 2, and the untouched ones 0. An untouched island therefore never ends ranked 4 or 5. The rank table, not the shares, is what the rest of the turn reads — placement and the attack weighting alike.
  - **The candidate scores.** The front-line countries are shuffled uniformly, and each is scored by its island's rank together with a one-off ownership indicator (1 when the country's island is wholly the computer's, else 0): rank 5 (only reachable on an island it does not own completely) scores 10 plus a border term — 1 for bordering an enemy, raised to 3 by a 7-in-8 draw when that enemy border is on the same island, so 10, 11 or 13 — with a 1-in-14 draw zeroing the term; rank 4 scores 5 plus 3 for a same-island enemy border plus the indicator (5 or 8 when the island is not wholly owned, 6 when it is); rank 3 scores 2 plus the indicator (2 or 3); ranks 2 and 0 score the indicator alone (0 or 1). The five highest scores, taken in order with ties broken by the shuffle, form the preferred list; a 1-in-10 draw then swaps the top two when the second holds more armies than the first. With fewer than five front-line countries the preferred list repeats entries.
  - **The island-defence branches.** For each island the computer owns completely, a per-island case adds countries to a seeding list: the countries added are exactly that island's key countries (the 23-country key list, up to three per island — table under Observations), each added when a named threat stands (an enemy stack across the water, with a 0-or-1 adjustment added to the threat's stack — drawn once for the island's branch and reused across its tests — compared with the defended country's own stack, or an enemy holding a named adjacent country, or the defended stack being below the branch's 6-to-9 threshold while a named threat is larger). A country can be added twice (its threat test and its small-stack test can both fire), which doubles its seed. One slip is worth naming: in the island-7 case, the second test for country 36 reads country 34's stack where the threshold comparison wants 36's. The branches run only while the island is wholly the computer's, and all but the island-4 and island-8 cases are additionally gated on some neighbouring island not being wholly the computer's.
  - **The production-centre branch.** On a 1-in-3 draw — suppressed entirely when the computer owns no island completely — each of the three production-centre countries the computer owns is added to the seeding list on its own 1-in-2 draw.
  - **The spending.** The handler first places exactly one army into each seeded country, in list order, while the pool lasts; this one-army pass runs on a draw of 1 to 8 being below 8 (7 in 8), but when the seeding list holds more than 11 countries the draw is replaced by 5 plus a draw of 1 to 3 — 6, 7 or 8 — so the pass then runs only on the 6 and the 7 (2 times in 3), not always. The stored stack is capped at 999 like the human's. The remainder — which is most of the pool whenever the seeding list is short, and all of it when the pass is skipped or nothing was seeded — goes by drawn rules to the preferred list: a draw of 1 (of 10) gives a third to the third-ranked preferred country, then half of what remains to the second, then the whole rest to the top; a draw of 2 halves it between the second and the top; draws of 3 to 10 give everything to the top country (forced when fewer than 4 armies remain); a 1-in-18 draw instead gives everything to one country drawn from the top five and a 1-in-20 draw to any front-line country, the 20 checked after the 18 so it replaces it, and a pool below 4 forces the everything-to-the-top case and replaces both. The crediting loop then adds each prepared amount to its country's stored stack (capped at 999) without subtracting it from the pool global — F013's finding, confirmed: the pool is fully credited onto countries, and the global itself is not reliably zeroed at return.
- **Same rule?** No. The human places the same whole pool, but by hand, on any of its own countries in any split (F013, Q-REI-03); the computer's countries are chosen by this scoring and its splits by these draws. Both cap the stored stack at 999, and neither carries armies over.

### Attack: when it attacks and how it picks

#### Q-AI-01 When and wherefrom does the computer decide to attack, and how does it choose the target?

- **As read (about 90%).** The decision sits in the computer's turn frame, after placement and before the move (F012's order, confirmed here). The frame draws the number of attack rounds: 2 to 5 (a draw of 1 to 4 plus one), plus 1 when the seat holds more than 16 countries, plus 3 more above 25, plus 4 when the game's fast-forward flag stands — which the info rebuild sets exactly when no human seat has any country left and the elimination prompt has not silenced it, so in the unregistered build the +4 applies from the turn after the human's elimination (see Q-AI-02 for the flag's mechanism). When the seat holds fewer than 8 countries the number is replaced by 2 to 4 and every addition, the +4 included, is dropped with it. A separate draw of 1 to 22 skips the whole attack block when it lands on 22 — but only while the seat holds 18 or fewer countries, above which the draw is forced past the skip value and the block always runs. Each round calls the attack builder twice: once outright, and a second time while the computer has captured nothing this turn and holds more than 8 countries. The builder rebuilds the candidate list from scratch each call: every front-line country whose armies strictly exceed a floor — a fresh draw of 2 or 3, fixed at 3 once the computer has captured this turn, one higher for the 23 key countries — is matched against each of its enemy neighbours whose armies are smaller by a margin of 0 or 1 drawn once for that candidate source and reused for all its enemy neighbours (not drawn per neighbour). Every acceptable pair enters the list. This is F011's gate reading, confirmed exactly. The picker then draws one pair uniformly from the list — the same-island weighting of Q-AI-06 has already multiplied the entries — records the strongest other enemy neighbour of the chosen attacker as its stop limit, and starts the battle: F011's passes, exchange rule and stop tests (capture; armies below 2 plus a fresh draw of 1 or 2; key attacker that has captured and holds fewer than 4; armies above 5 but below the strongest other enemy neighbour), confirmed here. When the builder finds no acceptable pair it raises its stop flag, and no further round attacks; the frame moves on.

### The scoring behind the choices

#### Q-AI-06 How are candidate attack pairs and placement countries scored and ranked?

- **As read (about 85%).** One rank table, built during placement, serves both decisions. The island shares (the computer's hold of each island, over its complete country list) are turned into ranks 5 to 2 by the four fixed passes of Q-AI-05, with the fold-back cases stated there. Placement reads the ranks through the candidate scores (13/11/10; 8, 6 or 5; 3 or 2; 1/0). The rank-4 island is always one the computer holds countries in: an untouched island ends at rank 2, 3 or 0 (Q-AI-05), and a pair's attacker must be an owned country, so no same-island pair can sit on an island the computer holds nothing of. The picks themselves are uniform draws over the weighted list; no score is computed at attack time beyond the gates.

### Moving after the attacks

#### Q-AI-04 When does the computer not attack, and when and how does it move after its attacks?

- **As read (about 85%).** A computer turn with no attack happens in exactly these ways: the 1-in-22 skip while it holds 18 or fewer countries; every round finding no acceptable pair (no front-line country at all — an interior holding — or nothing passing the floor and margin gates); or the game being over (each step is gated on the game-over flag, F012). The move comes after all attack rounds, once per turn, and only when the game is not over. The move handler first builds the pool of eligible sources: the computer's own countries holding more than 2 armies, with no enemy neighbour, that are not key countries — an interior, safe, big stack. (The handler also computes, per country, its largest enemy neighbour's stack with a slip that re-reads the first border slot's country for the second to fourth slots; the slip cannot change the selection, because a country with no enemy neighbour has a zero figure and a country with one is excluded anyway.) It takes the largest such stack and looks up its destination in a hard-coded table over exactly the 23 non-key countries — the complement of the key list, country by country — each entry pointing at a neighbouring country (one step toward the map's front lines); five of the entries draw among two or three destinations (sources 2, 6, 11, 19, 20), and four compare the destinations' current stacks (sources 18, 25, 35, 39), so a country not in the table never moves by this table path. When the table gives no destination, five 1-in-7 special walks can still fire, each gated on owning the islands around a specific corridor and each moving a key country: the walks move the stacks on country 26, 32, 15 or 37 (each only above 7 armies) one step along its corridor, and — with no army condition at all — the stack on country 5 to country 7 when islands 1, 2 and 3 are all owned; with a 0- or 1-army stack on country 5 this last walk can drain one army from the destination, a corner the table path cannot reach (its sources hold more than 2). So the handler as a whole does not forbid key sources: it is the table, not the handler, that excludes them. Whatever move was decided is cancelled 7 times in 8 when the source country is one of the production centres. The move itself transfers all but one army (the destination capped at 999 in storage); the source is left holding exactly 1. This is F011's "all but one, once, at the end" with the policy now read.
- **Same rule?** No: the human chooses source, destination and amount (1 to armies minus 1); the computer's source is usually its largest safe interior stack (or a key country via one of the five corridor fallbacks), its destination is fixed or drawn, and its amount is fixed at all but one.

### Same dice, same income, own choices

#### Q-AI-02 Does the computer earn and roll like the human, and how does its card play differ?

- **As read (income and dice about 90%, the card priority about 95%, the absence of a difficulty term about 85%).** Income is one shared handler for all seats: countries divided by 3, floored at 2 on that part alone, plus the island bonuses — no seat term, no difficulty term, no centre term (F013's reading; re-confirmed here: the computer's placement head only adds the stored trade-in or doubles the pool, never recomputes). The dice are the same single-die exchanges in both handlers, attacker winning only on a strictly higher die, one army to the loser per exchange (F011); the one mechanical difference is the five-exchange batching threshold, which stands at more than 40 against more than 15 for the computer and more than 35 against more than 15 for the human. No handler of the computer's turn — placement, attacks, battle, move, card check — reads the difficulty value (a search over all 213 decompiled scripts for the level's every reader; see the difficulty section). The card play differs only in choice, not in value: the computer's check counts wildcards toward each tradeable kind with the same cap of two as the human's, then takes at most one set by the fixed priority — the +15 kind, the +8 kind, the doubling kind, the +5 kind, the bomb match — confirming F013's and F014's priority from the full reading; the counters are cleared after each priority branch, so later branches cannot fire once one has (the first match in priority order is the only one taken, and a match found is never skipped). The removal walks the hand in slot order and clears the first three cards that are the chosen kind or a wildcard, with no cap on the wildcards it consumes — F014's corner, confirmed. The stored effect is applied by the placement head (add, double, or bomb). The full-hand discard before a draw runs by the fixed priority anti-bomb, bomb-match card, +5 card, else a random slot (F014, confirmed from the draw handler). Behaviourally F011's statement is right; the mechanism is this flag, not a seat count.

### What the difficulty setting changes

- **As read (about 90%).** The level (the options screen's strength value) is read by exactly five places outside its own option handler: the two options-screen display frames (`BehaviorScript 457` and `BehaviorScript 479`, both `exitFrame`, which only set level-named cast sprites), the defaults handler that loads, validates and saves it, the score writer that routes wins and losses into per-level slots, and the deal handler. No computer handler reads it: not the turn frame, not the placement, not the attack builders, not the dice, not the move — so nothing in Q-AI-01, Q-AI-02, Q-AI-04, Q-AI-05 or Q-AI-06 varies with the level. Its only in-game effect is in the deal: the handler fixes, per level, a guaranteed minimum of starting countries for each human seat — 5 at value 1, 9 at value 2 (the default), 14 at value 3, 16 at value 4 — by overwriting that many of the 46 dealt entries with the seat's number before the shuffle (so the seat ends with at least that many, and the computer seats share the rest). Values 5 to 10 have no branch in the deal handler. The option button sets only values 1 to 3 (the hard entry sets 1, the normal entry 2, the easy entry 3); the defaults handler accepts 1 to 10 from the defaults file with no registration gate (a hand-edited file can therefore select 4, whose branch exists, or 5 to 10, whose behaviour at runtime is not established — the unregistered path forces the seat layout, the event frequency and the starting armies but not the level). F011's Q-SET-04 list of controls and F013's placement reading are consistent with this: the level buys the human a bigger or smaller share of the board, and nothing else.

## Method

- **Artifact and tools.** The decompiled scripts of F010's run `pro-r1` (ProjectorRays 0.2.0 on the movie of `Islepro.exe`, sha256 above), read in place on the owner's machine in the folder `$IWA_STATIC` names; nothing was copied into the repository (rule 18). The scripts' hashes (`ls_sha256`) are those of `experiments/E008-static-triage/measurements/iwp-extract-pro-r1.json`; E014 lists the 21 scripts read with them, and `experiments/E014-pro-ai-reading/check_cited_scripts.py` checks that this finding cites no script, name or handler the record does not have, and (with `--disk`) that each listed script on disk, hashed in place, has the record's hash.
- **What was read.** In full, in order: the computer's placement handler (all 903 lines — the trade-in head, the bomb branch, the island-share build and rank passes, the candidate scoring and preferred list, the nine island-defence branches, the production-centre branch, the seed loop, the remainder splits and the final crediting loop); the attack builder (including the whole weighting pass, twice); the candidate-list handler; the attack picker; the computer's dice handler (the battle loop and the complete capture-split cascade, branch by branch); the move handler (the source pool, the whole 23-entry destination table, the five special walks, the centre veto, the transfer); the computer's card-match check; the computer's draw handler; the computer's turn frame; the movie-start handler's board, island, key-country and per-country island tables; the deal handler's strength branches and their place in the deal; the info rebuild's fast-forward write; the defaults handler (both handlers); the score writer's level branches; the strength option button; the two options-screen display frames; the speed option button; and the elimination prompt. Bytecode comparisons: the turn frame's round number (the draw of 4 plus 1, the additions of 1, 3 and 4 as immediates, the flag test before the 4, the draw of 22, the forcing of 8 above 18, the draw of 3 plus 1 under 8) and the placement handler's draw bounds (8, 14, 10, 4+5, 2, 3, and the 18/20 pair) were each confirmed in the `.lasm` listings.
- **What was searched, in the terminal only and with nothing kept.** Every writer of the rank table, the preferred list and the aliased country list (the placement handler only, and the candidate-list handler's aliasing); every writer of the fast-forward flag (the start, deal, reset and end-frame paths set 0; one writer sets 1); every reader of the level value (the five places named above, and no computer handler); every caller of the six computer handlers (one caller each, in the turn frame or the attack chain); the writers of the animation-length global (the speed option and the defaults, never the AI).

The human's placement, combat and card paths beyond what F011 to F014 state; the deal's full arithmetic (T18); the centre events (T23). Where an answer needs the frame order it says so.

### The key-country list (VIPC) and where it enters

23 countries, up to three per island, built once at movie start (the zero is padding on the three-country islands): island 1 — countries 1, 3; island 2 — 5, 7, 8; island 3 — 9, 13; island 4 — 15, 14, 23; island 5 — 24, 26; island 6 — 27, 32, 33; island 7 — 34, 36; island 8 — 37, 38, 43; island 9 — 44, 45, 46 (the whole island). It enters the AI in six places: the attack floor is one higher from these countries; the same countries are the ones the island-defence branches seed; a key attacker's capture branch sets an intermediate keep of 3 at home, which the later isolated-capture rules and the final strongest-enemy override can replace; a captured key country receives a garrison of 3 only in the isolated case the final override does not replace; the move handler's destination table covers exactly the other 23, so keys are not table sources — but its five corridor fallbacks move from key countries 26, 32, 15, 37 and 5, so the handler does not forbid keys as sources; and the stop test that ends a key attacker's battle below 4 armies after a capture is F011's. The placement seeds and the move table are thus two views of one list.

### Agreement with the findings

- **F011 (combat):** the gates, margins, floors, stops, batching and no-forfeit are confirmed exactly, including the bytecode-confirmed comparisons it names. Two refinements, not contradictions: the +4 attack rounds' condition is the fast-forward flag (which is set exactly when no human seat is left, so F011's wording holds behaviourally for the unregistered build, but the +4 is dropped by the under-8 replacement exactly as the size additions are); and once a round finds no acceptable pair, all remaining rounds are skipped, not just that one.
- **F013 (reinforcement):** the whole pool is credited and the pool global is not reliably zeroed — confirmed. Refined: the island shares behind the placement priorities are computed over the computer's complete country list (F013's wording attached them to the owned-islands list, which in fact drives only the defence branches); the seed pass is a single one-army pass over the seeding list, after which the drawn splits move the bulk of the pool (F013's "one army at a time or in splits" is the same mechanism seen from the tail); and the "third and a half" split is one of five drawn remainder rules.
- **F014 (cards):** the priority, the wildcard cap on detection, the uncapped removal, the discard priority and the bomb branch (including the index quirk) are confirmed exactly; no disagreement found in the full reading of either script.

### Agreement with the documents

The captured help text (F003) describes no targeting, placement or movement rule for the computer, and the directions (F004) say only that the computer plays the other three seats; there is nothing to contradict. malpaco's `RULES.md` AI rows are guesses by design; the readings here are the first attributed source for Pro's machine: islands ranked by share, key countries defended and seeded (and mostly excluded from the move table, though the corridor fallbacks can still move them), a floor-and-margin attack gate, drawn rounds, and a level that only resizes the human's deal.

## Inferences

- **Several of the machine's decisions are fixed or coarse, and the reading states them without judging them.** Nothing in its code sees more than the human's code sees; the reading's list of fixed or near-fixed rules is: the 1-in-10 swap of its top two placement destinations, the rank fold-back that can rank its best island 2 or 3 early, the trade-in priority that takes 15 armies over doubling (F013's), the bomb pick that compares army-table entries by index (F014's), and a move table that walks exactly one step per turn plus the five corridor fallbacks. How any of this affects the quality of its play is not a static question; it is T28's and malpaco's to measure. From Q-AI-05, Q-AI-06, Q-AI-04.
- **The key list is the AI's spine.** Attack floors, defence seeding, capture keeps and garrisons, and the move table all hang off the same 23 countries; the move table is their exact complement, with the five corridor fallbacks as the exception that still lets them move. Malpaco can treat "key countries" as one configurable table and reproduce most of the machine's shape from it. From the Observations table.
- **The level is not a difficulty knob for the AI.** It resizes the human's opening (5/9/14 countries of 46 — a spread of about a fifth of the board) and nothing else. A malpaco difficulty control that changes the AI's aggression would be a different design. From the difficulty section.
- **The +4 rounds arrive only when the game is already decided.** With no human seat left, a computer holding at least 8 countries gains four attack rounds and loses the animation delays; a computer below 8 countries has its whole round total replaced, so the +4 and the size additions do not reach it. From Q-AI-02.

## Alternatives considered

- **The rank fold-back might be intended.** Not decidable from code: the four fixed passes with sentinel marks read as a hand-rolled "rank the top four" whose edge behaviour follows mechanically. The finding states what the code does; intent is not claimed.
- **The +4 might belong to the speed setting.** Excluded: the speed option writes the animation-length global, never the flag; the flag's only setter is the no-human condition; the bytecode shows the flag test immediately before the addition.
- **The move table's complement with the key list might be coincidence.** The complement is exact (23 sources, 23 keys, no overlap); the exclusion is also enforced by the handler's own test, so the behaviour holds whether or not the author meant the pairing.
- **The key-attacker capture branch's inner draws might matter.** Traced branch by branch: the branch's random draw of 3 to 5 is dead, because the keep-3 reset that follows it (or the branch's own entry) always overwrites it; and the branch's keep-3 is itself intermediate — its own isolated-capture draw of 1 to 3 can replace it, and the isolated-capture rules and the final strongest-enemy override after the branch can replace it again. Stated as read, with the trace behind it.
- **The island-7 threshold slip might be a decompiler error.** Possible but uncorrectable here: one decompiler, and the bytecode listing shows the same country read. The effect is bounded (one of the two tests for country 36 uses the neighbouring stack as the threshold reference).
- **A second AI (an easier mode) might hide in the unwritten scripts.** Not excluded with certainty (61 scripts with no cast member, F010); every computer handler the turn frame calls was read, and the frame's calls are the only ones (searched).

## What this does not establish

- **That the decompiler is faithful in every branch.** One decompiler (ProjectorRays 0.2.0); the bytecode listings corroborate the draws and the turn-frame arithmetic named in the Method, and F011's listings corroborate the combat comparisons; no run corroborates any behaviour.
- **Runtime behaviour and the score.** The frame order rests on the jumps each script makes (F012's method); Director's resolution of competing jumps, its float-max behaviour on the share list, and the bomb pick's out-of-range index (F014's open point) are runtime questions.
- **The registered build,** and anything gated by the upgrade check (rule 19). The level's defaults-file path is not gated — read as written — but what registration changes elsewhere is `unknown`.
- **The quality of the play.** A reading says what the code does, not whether it plays well; that is T28's and malpaco's to measure. No win rate, no aggression measure, no skill claim is made (Q-AI-07 stays a result question).
- **The played frequencies** of every drawn choice: the draw ranges are the code's; how often each branch fires in play is T28's.
- **The deal's arithmetic** beyond the level minimum (T18), the centre events (T23), the card kinds and deck (T22), the human's paths (F011 to F014), and DOS Isle Wars (rule 19).
